Project Objective

With the adoption of the da Vinci SP at Stanford Medicine, Dr. Holsinger's team found themselves with an ever-growing stream of high-quality 4K surgical video that was largely underutilized. ViGOR is a vision-language framework that automates operative note generation directly from surgical video.

Project Overview

ViGOR identifies key procedural events and translates them into professional clinical narratives using a 3-stage pipeline: computer vision, information extraction, and natural language generation. This process replaces traditional, memory-based dictation with a verifiable draft grounded in visual evidence.

1 / Background & Motivation

Improving Clinical Accuracy

Operative notes written hours after procedure are subject to recall bias, omission, and compression. A system that reconstructs events directly from video produces a note anchored to visual evidence.

Reducing Administrative Burden

Documentation represents a significant and growing share of a surgeon's non-clinical time. Automating the first draft of an operative note could meaningfully reduce that burden, freeing capacity for direct patient care.

Harnessing the da Vinci SP

Robotic surgical systems such as the da Vinci SP generate immense amounts of stabilized 4K video. While this sheer volume of unstructured data is difficult to process manually, it provides the perfect, highly-controlled environment for computer vision research.

2 / Challenges

The Stanford Dataset

Initiated in collaboration with Dr. Holsinger at Stanford University, our project leverages a dataset created by his clinical team. The initial dataset included 62 de-identified roboticsurgical videos paired with human-dictated operative notes. However, many cases featured legacy user interfaces or were incomplete with key procedural elements unrecorded. To ensure a robust and standardized training environment, we implemented rigorous inclusion criteria, narrowing our scope from 62 to 36 relevant cases.

Phase Classification

For training, each video was expert-labeled across nine predefined surgical phases. Our initial strategy centered on training a phase classification model. We aimed to utilize a Vision Transformer (ViT) to map frames to one of nine phases. The goal was to build a pipeline capable of identifying clinical milestones. By providing the model with these specific labels, we expected to group frames by assigned phase before passing them to a language model for clinical caption generation.

Label Sparsity and Non-Linear Flow

As we began model development, we encountered two fundamental issues. Firstly, expert surgeons assigned phase labels to only 35% of the video frames, leaving 65% as non-operative "dead time" consisting of transitions and instrument cleaning. Secondly, the procedure exhibited non-linear progression, where surgeons would repeat or skip steps. It became clear that forcing a chronological checklist onto messy video data was insufficient for clinical accuracy.

3 / Discovery

While working with surgeons to label training data, we noticed a consistent pattern: labels were more tied to instrument activity rather than elapsed time. This led us to shift our focus from frame-level classification to segmentation. We developed two custom tools to confirm this hypothesis and help the surgeons label more efficiently and accurately.

Cautery Activity as Signal

To test our observation, we built a diagnostic tool to read instrument activity directly from the da Vinci’s Head-Up Display (HUD). This system extracted real-time signals of when the surgeon engaged the cautery pedals. Analyzing the data revealed a 90% correlation between these activation triggers and the segments experts deemed clinically significant. By focusing on these active beats, we could ignore 65% of the visual noise and prioritize frames with the highest information density.

Action Segmentation

Following this insight, we developed an Action Segmentation Tool to group cautery activity into segments. We found 30-seconds was an ideal window to account for tool repositioning and brief pauses in activity. Instead of requiring experts to scrub through hours of raw footage, this tool presented them with pre-filtered action segments for labeling. This new approach yielded a five-fold reduction in expert labeling time while also improving consistency across the team of experts.

4 / Vision-Language Architecture

Stage 1. Visual Encoding

The foundation of the architecture is the DINOv3 encoder, which utilizes a patchwork attention mechanism to process the video frames. We found this general-purpose model superior to surgical models pre-trained on datasets like Cholec80, which did not translate to the unique robotic endoscopy environment.

Stage 2. Temporal Segmentation

We then use a Multi-Stage Temporal Convolutional Network (MS-TCN) to infer action segments, creating a structured timeline of video segments. Segments are then passed as keyframes constructed into mosaics to the language models.

Stage 3: Two-Pass Narrative Synthesis

The final stage uses a two-pass strategy. In Pass 1, the system identifies instruments and actions within each segment to provide key observations. In Pass 2, these observations are synthesized into a structured operative note. To ensure clinical accuracy, we developed an interface that allows surgeons to see the exact video mosaic that validates each observation.

5 / Outcomes & Learnings

ViGOR serves as a successful proof-of-concept for how both computer vision and natural language processing can be applied to complex industries like medicine, especially in the case of robotic surgery. The results were highly encouraging, as surgeons graded the generated notes an average of 85% when compared to the human-authored notes. In 21% of evaluated cases, the experts actually rated the generated notes to be equal to or better than the official dictations.

The success of the project was ultimately enabled by the collaborative relationship we built with the surgeons. By approaching them as partners rather than stakeholders, we were able to translate their clinical expertise into technical solutions and find compelling discoveries in the field.
